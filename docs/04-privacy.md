# Keeping Living Relatives' Details Private

*This chapter protects real people — your relatives, and you. Family history
projects that share a living person's address or phone number "because the
book is only for family" can still leak, once, forever.*

## The distinction that governs everything

- **Deceased** relatives' facts: generally fine for your family book. This is
  the standard genealogical practice, and their stories are your material.
- **Living** relatives' facts: private by default. Publish only with their
  explicit, specific, informed consent.

"Living" includes people who are confirmed alive *and* people you cannot
confirm are deceased. If you're unsure a person is alive, treat them as
living. When in doubt, ask the family or leave the detail out.

## What is sensitive about the living

More than you'd think. Beyond the obvious, these all deserve protection:

- Home addresses, emails, and phone numbers (including *new* ones — they help
  find people today).
- Exact birth dates and birthplaces, which fuel identity fraud and are gold
  for account-recovery guesses.
- Medical or mental-health details, diagnoses, and treatments. This manual
  is not medical guidance, and a family book is the wrong venue anyway.
- Immigration status, legal troubles, divorce and custody details,
  employment problems, debts.
- Photos of children, and children's full names, ages, or schools.
- Anything a relative asked you not to share. "Asked once in 2019" still
  counts.

Note that even deceased-people stories often include living people: *"Aunt
Maya's son Dave…"* may name a living person. Read shared stories with that
lens before publishing.

## Say yes to: the safe categories

You want the book to feel warm and alive, not redacted to rubble. These are
almost always fine:

- Stories (as consented) told *about* the living by those willing.
- First names with general contexts: "my cousin Dave joined us every
  summer."
- Decades-old childhood anecdotes the subject happily re-tells at weddings.
- Group photos where the living appear, if the photo's subject is the past
  and everyone in it consented (or happily agrees today).

The tone to aim for: the book is a **public-facing, keep-forever artifact**.
Write every line as if it will be read by the person it names. Comfortable?
Publish. Not comfortable? Redact or ask first.

## The consent script (adapt, don't legalize)

For living relatives you plan to name or show:

> *"I'm putting together our family book — the one with Grandpa's bakery
> stories. It mentions [you / Dave] in the summer-cabin chapter and shows the
> 1985 group photo. I'd rather ask than assume: are you comfortable being
> named, shown in the photo, or both? If you'd rather I use just 'a cousin'
> or leave it out, say the word and I'll handle it — no hard feelings at
> all."*

Then log it. The [privacy log](../worksheets/privacy-log.html) records who
consented to what, when. Verbal consent is fine; write it down right away
("Maya, 2026-10-11: ok with cabin chapter, prefers no birth date").

Respect changes of mind. A relative who enjoyed being named in 2026 may
reconsider in 2028; honor the new answer and fix the files.

## Practical redaction routine

Before anything leaves your folder — the book, a shared PDF, a photo album
gift:

1. Do a **living-pass**: skim every page, caption, and photo note, asking
   "is a living person named, dated, addressed, or diagnosed here?"
2. Redact by replacing, not starring. `1974-03-03` becomes `born in the
   1970s`; phone numbers and addresses become story-absent (they don't
   belong in a memoir); "my sister" is often warmer than her name anyway.
3. Record any redaction in the privacy log — so Future You remembers it was
   a deliberate choice, not an oversight.
4. For the *family-only* printed copy you may keep some living details
   (grandkids' names in a dedication, say) *if* copy recipients are the
   consent-givers. That copy is still private: mark it clearly
   "FAMILY COPY — DO NOT REDISTRIBUTE" on the inside page.

## AI and privacy: three hard rules

1. **Don't paste living relatives' details into any AI tool.** Names, birth
   dates, addresses, diagnoses: no. When asking an AI to help redact a
   family tree or chapter, first replace real names with labels (`REL_1`,
   `REL_2`) — or keep the work entirely in your head/local tools.
2. **Prefer local options for sensitive files.** A transcription tool that
   runs on your computer keeps the audio on your computer. When you have
   privacy-sensitive material (recordings of the living describing living
   people), choose tools with local processing, or transcribe by hand.
3. **Never ask an AI to store "family context" across sessions** in ways you
   can't inspect. Keep the master family data in *your* files, not in a
   chat app's memory. Any account you use for this project should have
   two-factor authentication turned on, and nothing about your family stored
   in its chat history that you'd be upset to have reviewed later.

## Privacy in the finished book, summarized

| Detail | Default |
| --- | --- |
| Deceased people, their dates and stories | Include |
| Living people, their stories (consented) | Include, as consented |
| Living people, names/dates | First name at most; dates out |
| Any postal address, email, or phone | Never |
| Medical or legal details of anyone | Never |
| Children's full names, ages, schools | Never (initials at most, if at all) |
| "FAMILY COPY" extras | Consented recipients only, marked, tracked |

If a line violates this table, it doesn't go to print. Warm books and
private people are not in conflict — the warmth survives on stories, and
stories are what this manual is for.
