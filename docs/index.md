## Who this is for

You have a box of photographs, a grandmother with a hundred stories, and a
sneaking suspicion that if you don't write any of this down soon, some of it
will be lost forever. You also have an AI assistant that seems almost too good
to be true — and that instinct is correct, in the most useful way.

This manual is for people who want to capture their family's history with the
help of AI tools **without** letting the AI make things up, **without**
exposing living relatives' private information, and **without** losing their
own voice in the process.

It's written like a friend would explain it: plain English, step by step,
never condescending. If you've never used an AI tool or touched a scanner,
you're in the right place. If you're a seasoned researcher, the checklists and
worksheets will still earn their keep.

## The one rule that runs everything

> **Treat every AI output as a rough draft from an enthusiastic, occasionally
> forgetful friend — and verify anything that matters against a real record.**

AI is wonderful at the boring parts: transcribing an interview, suggesting
interview questions, cleaning up a scan's filename, summarizing a messy note.
AI is *not* wonderful at knowing whether your grandfather actually served on
the USS Kilty, or whether Great Aunt Rosa was born in 1902 or 1903. It will
answer with confidence either way. That confidence is not evidence.

Every chapter in this manual assumes the AI proposes and **you** verify.

## The five habits that make this project succeed

1. **Record everything you can.** Audio of a story beats a perfect summary of
   the story. Words from the source are gold; your retelling of them is
   secondhand.
2. **Name things immediately.** Files, scans, recordings, drafts — name them
   the day you make them, using the conventions in the Photos chapter.
   Someday Future You will be very grateful.
3. **Verify before you write.** Never pour an AI-generated fact into your
   manuscript until a real source backs it. The Verification chapter shows
   you exactly how, in about two minutes per fact.
4. **Protect the living.** Names, addresses, and details of living relatives
   stay out of the shared book unless those relatives said yes.
5. **Keep your voice.** AI drafts are scaffolding, not the building. The
   Writing chapter shows how to use AI without sounding like AI.

## What you need

Almost nothing. This guide is deliberately **local-first**:

- A computer (any laptop from the last decade).
- A phone with a camera and voice recorder — that's your scanner and audio rig.
- Optionally, a flatbed scanner (a used one works great).
- Optionally, a free AI assistant (chat or local) for transcription,
  question-bank help, and drafting support.

Nothing here requires a paid subscription, a cloud account, or sending your
family photos to a server on the other side of the world. Work in a folder on
your computer; back it up to a second drive; sleep well.

## A warm word before you start

Family history projects fail for one reason more than any other: they feel
enormous, so they never start. This manual breaks the work into small,
finishable chunks — one interview, one folder of scans, one chapter. Do one
small piece this week. In a year you'll have a book. Starting is the whole
trick. You've already done it by reading this far.
