# Verifying Every AI Claim Against Real Records

*AI can propose a hundred facts a minute. Records can confirm them at your
pace. This chapter is the spine of the whole manual: it's how your book earns
the trust of people who read it after you're gone.*

## The core problem, plainly

AI language tools sometimes produce facts that are wrong while sounding
exactly as confident as facts that are right. Researchers call this "hallucination";
in plain English, **an AI will guess rather than admit it doesn't know, and
its guesses look like knowledge.**

If your family book says Grandpa sailed on the USS *Kilty* and the AI invented
that ship, you've placed a false fact right where it's hardest to remove —
inside a book your grandchildren will treat as truth. So:

> **Rule: nothing an AI told you goes into the book until you've seen it in
> a real source.** The AI can find, propose, and draft. Only records,
> interviews, and original documents can *confirm*.

## What counts as a "real record"

Stronger sources live at the top:

| Strength | Source | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best | Original documents in your hand | The ship's log, the deed, the letters |
| Great | Certified copies / digitized originals | Census pages, passenger lists, vital records |
| Good | Published index transcripts of the above | Database entries indexed from archives |
| Weaker | Family memory | Aunt Maya recalls the bakery opened in 1948 |
| Weakest | AI output, anything unsourced | "He likely served in the Navy" |

Notice family memory sits below documents but far above AI output: memory got
you into the archive in the first place. AI output is not a source at all —
it's a **lead**, a hint about where to look.

## Types of records, by what they can prove

- **Who someone is:** birth, marriage, death records; baptism records; family
  Bibles with written entries.
- **Where someone lived:** censuses (every ten years, most countries),
  city directories, property records.
- **Work and service:** military records, union ledgers, employment letters,
  membership cards, apprenticeship papers.
- **Travel:** passenger manifests, naturalization papers, passports.
- **Daily life:** letters, diaries, receipts, newspapers (search the local
  paper for any name in your tree — obituaries were long, detailed, and full
  of names).

Free starting points include national archives, state/county record offices,
public library card databases, FamilySearch (free account), and local
newspaper archives. Many libraries offer free home access to big newspaper
databases with just a library card.

## The 4-step verification routine (about two minutes per fact)

Every time AI (or anyone) offers a fact:

1. **Write the claim down** in your [source log](../worksheets/source-log.html)
   with the word `UNVERIFIED` next to it. A fact that isn't logged gets lost.
2. **Name the record that could prove it.** "Claim: Joe Smith served on USS
   Kilty. Record: military service record / muster rolls." If you can't name
   a record type, the claim can't be verified yet — park it.
3. **Find the record.** Use the AI to search that type of source, or ask a
   librarian. Confirm the *specific detail* (date, ship, name spelling), not
   merely something nearby.
4. **Log the source or kill the claim.** In the source log: the record's
   title, where you saw it, the date you checked. If you can't confirm it,
   either cut it from the book or write it with its doubt attached:
   "Maya says the bakery opened in 1948; city records show the license in
   1951."

## How to read a citation (someone else's, and your own)

When anyone — a database, a relative, an AI — gives you a fact, ask three
questions:

- **Where did they get it?** (No answer = not a source.)
- **Did they see it or hear about it?** (Saw beats heard-about, every time.)
- **Can I see it too?** (If not, treat it as a strong rumor and keep the
  search open.)

This is also the standard for your own book's citations. You don't need
academic formatting; you need "Aunt Maya, interview audio 01 at 14:30" and
"1920 U.S. Census, Marion County, page 4" — exact enough that a reader
(probable grandchild) could find it again.

## Handling contradictions — the fun part

Records contradict each other a lot: a census enumerator wrote down what a
neighbor guessed; a birth date was fudged on a marriage license. When sources
disagree:

1. Prefer the record made **closest in time to the event** by someone with
   direct knowledge.
2. Prefer the **original** over a transcription.
3. Note the conflict in your book rather than silently picking one:
   "Rosa's headstone says 1902; the 1910 census agrees; her wedding record
   says 1904, likely a bride trimming her age."

Family historians get excited by contradictions, because reconciling them
often uncovers the true story.

## Useful AI checks you *can* trust (with care)

AI is a fine partner for the *mechanics* of verification:

- **Search strategy:** "What record type would prove someone's 1930 Indiana
   address?" (a librarian may still confirm the advice).
- **Transcribing** a scanned census image to text for your source log —
   check the numbers against the image; transposition errors are common.
- **Summarizing** an obituary into your citation's key facts.
- **Explaining** a record type or a legal term from 1920 in plain English.

AI is a *bad* partner for supplying the underlying facts. If an AI hands you
names, dates, or "historic records" without a link you can open, assume it
invented them until a real archive says otherwise.

## The verification checklist, ready to use

A printable version lives at
[the verification checklist](../worksheets/verification-checklist.html).
Tape it to your desk; run it before any fact enters a chapter:

1. Wrote the claim + `UNVERIFIED` in my source log
2. Named the record type that could prove it
3. Found and actually looked at the record (not just a summary)
4. Logged the record title, repository, and date checked
5. Cut or flagged anything I couldn't confirm

Five boxes between you and a false family legend. That's the whole deal.
