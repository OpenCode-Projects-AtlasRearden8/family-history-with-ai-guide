# Writing Memoir Chapters in Your Own Voice

*The house of your family book is built from interviews, photos, and records
— but the rooms are your sentences. Here, AI is scaffolding, never the
builder.*

## Why your voice matters so much

Your grandchildren will hear this book in your voice. If a machine's
constructions ("navigating the tapestry of his formative years") replace
yours ("Grandpa smelled like pipe tobacco and rise-and-shine"), the book
becomes a stranger. The chapters you write in your own voice will be quoted
for generations; no motivational drift or "delve" leakage survives in those
pages.

The good news: writing in your own voice is *less* work with AI, not more —
because AI handles the organizing and polishing chores while you supply all
of the meaning.

## The chapter recipe (about two hours, spread over a week)

Each chapter gets this treatment. Work from an interview transcript, your
photos, and your source log.

### Step 1 — Pick the chapter's spine (15 min, no AI)

Ask of the material: **what is this chapter really about?** Not "Grandpa's
life," but: "The bakery as the family's rumor mill and dinner table."
One subject, one spine. Write it as a sentence at the top of a fresh page:

> *Spine: the bakery ran on gossip as much as bread — and everybody benefited.*

Every anecdote either supports the spine or waits for another chapter. This
one decision is what makes a chapter strong rather than a chronology.

### Step 2 — Gather your raw blocks (30 min)

Copy from your transcript the 5–12 moments that serve the spine: quotes,
scenes, one-line memories. Paste the *teller's actual words*. Skim your
caption files for photos with genuine stories. Keep each block short.

### Step 3 — Ask AI to organize, not write (15 min)

Give an assistant **only your blocks** (scrubbed of living relatives' details
per the [privacy chapter](04-privacy.html)) and a prompt like:

> *"These are story fragments from an interview about a family bakery.
> Don't write prose. Group them into a possible order for a chapter whose
> main point is: [your spine sentence]. Flag any gaps where a transition or
> detail seems missing. Keep every quote exactly as written."*

This is the sweet spot: AI is excellent at seeing structure in scattered
blocks, and its output is (safely) not prose at all.

### Step 4 — Write the first draft in your voice (45–60 min, no AI)

Write the chapter straight through, in your own tone, using the AI's
suggested order only where it helps. Practical voice rules:

- Write like you talk, minus the fillers. Short sentences welcome.
- One scene at a time: who, where, what happened, what it meant to you.
- Use "I" freely; use real sensory detail (smells, sounds, prices, weather)
  from the transcript — the crown jewels of interviews.
- When you quote a relative, quote **exactly**, and cite the interview:
  (Maya Smith, 2026-10-11 audio 01).
- Disconnect from the AI while drafting. Voice leaks in only when you're
  typing alone.

### Step 5 — The two-pass revision (30 min, with and without AI)

**Pass 1, you:** read the draft aloud, alone. Fix anything you stumble over,
cut anything the spine doesn't need, and make every paragraph's first
sentence pull the reader forward.

**Pass 2, AI, carefully:** paste your draft (privacy-scrubbed) and ask:

> *"This is a memoir chapter. Don't rewrite my sentences. Point out: (1) any
> paragraph that feels out of order, (2) any place I repeat information,
> (3) any unclear sentence. Be blunt. Don't touch my tone."*

Then *you* make every change. If the AI suggests rewording a sentence and
you agree, retype it in words you would have chosen. Never accept a
machine's phrasing into the manuscript — that's how the voice erodes,
twenty words at a time.

## A worked mini-example

**Blocks (from transcript):**
- "Every Sunday the whole block smelled like butter cakes."
- "Dad cracked eggs one-handed; nobody else in the family ever learned."
- "Mrs. Kelly next door collected day-old loaves for the church."

**AI's job:** suggests order + notes that the reader will wonder whom Dad
sold to most — a gap you can fill next interview.

**Your draft:**

> By Sunday morning the whole block smelled like our butter cakes. Dad
> cracked eggs one-handed over the big steel bowl — a trick nobody else in
> the family ever mastered, though Mom claims he cheated with two hands when
> he thought we weren't looking. Mrs. Kelly from next door came by each
> evening for the day-old loaves to take to church. Dad charged her half.
> He never said why. I think the bread was the sermon.

That is a family book. An AI could never produce "I think the bread was the
sermon," because it wasn't there. Every fact in that paragraph traces to a
transcript, per the [verification routine](03-verification.html) — and the
*feel* of it is unmistakably yours.

## What AI should and shouldn't do in this project

| Good AI task | Off-limits |
| --- | --- |
| Grouping, ordering, and spotting gaps in blocks | Writing paragraphs for you |
| Fact-checking support: transcribing records, summarizing sources | Supplying facts or quotes it can't source |
| Copyedit pointing-out (not doing) | Rewriting in its own voice |
| Generating interview follow-up questions | Inventing scenes, dialogue, or feelings |
| Checking consistency of dates across chapters | Choosing your anecdotes |

## Keep your voice, chapter after chapter

- Keep a one-page "voice list" of words and rhythms you like: the family
  nicknames, the jokes, the way Grandma said "sufficient." Re-read it before
  each writing session.
- When you reread a chapter months later, ensure you'd recognize the author.
  If a paragraph could have come from anyone, rewrite it in memory of the
  moment it captures.
- And protect the crown jewels: three interviews with real voice recordings
  beat ten polished chapters with none of the tellers left. Record today.
