# Interviewing Relatives

*The single richest source of family history is a living person's memory.
Everything else in this manual supports the conversations in this chapter.*

## Why interview first, before anything else

Photos and records can wait a season; memories cannot. Every family history
project should begin with conversations, because:

- Memories answer questions no record can (who, feelings, why).
- Sure memories fade, people move, and stories change with each telling.
- An interview often reveals which photos matter, saving you scanning time.
- Relatives who share stories early become allies who donate photos later.

The goal of an interview is not data. The goal is a **recorded story,
in the teller's own words**, with the details they consider important.

## Before you go: four preparations

### 1. Prep the questions (but expect to abandon them)

Your question list is a safety net, not a script. Bring these:

- The general question bank in [the interview worksheet](../worksheets/interview-questions.html).
- Five to ten specific questions keyed to this person: names you've found in
  photos, gaps in your timeline, family mysteries ("why did they leave
  Poland?").
- Follow-up phrases you'll use constantly: *"Tell me more about that,"*
  *"What happened next,"* *"What did that smell like?"* Sensory follow-ups
  unlock more memory than any clever question.

### 2. Sort your questions from easy to hard

Every interview has an arc. Open with warm, easy questions (childhood home,
school, first job). Save sensitive topics — arguments, deaths, family
fractures — for the second half, after trust is established. If the hard
question never gets an answer, you still leave with a full recording of easy
material.

### 3. Test your recording setup

A story you failed to record is a story lost twice. Before the visit:

- Record 30 seconds of test audio where you'll actually sit. Play it back.
- Use a phone in airplane mode (a call during "the flood story" is a classic
  tragedy) with a recording app you already know.
- Place the phone between you; if two people tell stories, consider two
  recorders.
- Bring a backup: a second phone, or a notebook. Old relatives and old
  batteries both fail gently; catch it early.

### 4. Get consent, warmly and briefly

Rights and respect aside, consented relatives tell better stories. Say
something like: *"Would it be okay if I recorded this so I can retell it
right? I'd love to share the final version with you and maybe the family."*
Note who agreed, and to what (see the [Privacy chapter](04-privacy.html) for
the details). If someone declines recording, take notes by hand — that's
still a good interview.

## During the interview

The mechanics are simple; the kindness is the work.

- **Say names out loud at the start**: "This is Dylan, recording with Aunt
  Maya, Sunday after lunch, at the house on Grant Street." Storage programs
  trim file names; the spoken lead-in survives every rename.
- **Silence is a tool.** After an answer, wait three seconds. People fill
  silence with the second thought, which is usually the better one.
- **Follow the story, not the list.** If she starts talking about the
  bakery her parents ran, that's the interview now. Your list can wait.
- **Ask about the small things.** What they ate on Sundays. What a day in
  school felt like. What songs they sang. Grandchildren in fifty years will
  crave exactly these details, which no census record ever captured.
- **Don't argue with memory.** If a date seems off, note your doubt and move
  on — verification (Chapter 3) happens later, with records, not with
  correction from you mid-story. Interviewing is for collecting, not editing.
- **Watch for fatigue.** Most great interviews are 45–90 minutes. End while
  everyone's still happy.

## Where AI genuinely helps (and where it doesn't)

Use AI as your assistant after the interview, for things like:

- **Transcribing** the recording. Free tools can turn an hour of audio into
  a text file locally, keeping your audio private. Even a rough transcript
  lets you search and quote.
- **Summarizing** a long transcript into topic chunks: "1940s farm years,"
  "the bakery," "meeting Uncle Joe."
- **Suggesting follow-up questions** for the *next* interview based on the
  transcript ("You mentioned 'the incident at the lake' — here are 8 ways to
  ask about it").
- **Drafting a timeline** from the transcript's mentions of years and events.

AI does **not** help by answering your family's questions for you. Do not ask
an AI "what ship did Grandpa serve on?" and expect truth; an AI will happily
guess a plausible ship name. If it wasn't in the interview or a record, it
doesn't go in your book. Chapter 3 handles all of this.

## After the interview: the 24-hour ritual

Within a day, while your memory is fresh and the recording is still fragile:

1. Copy the audio from phone to computer. Original files stay untouched
   forever.
2. Name it using the convention: `YYYY-MM-DD_last-name_audio_01.m4a`
   (e.g., `2026-10-11_smith_audio_01.m4a`).
3. Back it up to your second location (external drive or trusted backup).
4. Spend ten minutes writing, *from your own head*, a short "interview
   memory" note: what stories were told, what lit her up, what to pursue next
   visit. Your feelings are source data too.
5. Log the interview in your [source log](../worksheets/source-log.html).

You now hold the most valuable object in modern family history: a preserved
first-person story. Everything else in this manual — scans, records, chapters
— exists to carry that story.
